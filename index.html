Weekly tasks

The four tasks build on one another. If you choose your Week 1 policy well, it can carry you through to the final portfolio.

Week 110%

LPP Critical Reflection Paper #1

Choose one language policy that operates in your own institution, written or unwritten. Analyze it with Spolsky's three components and Ruiz's orientations, and argue what the analysis reveals about who holds language-policy power.

1,500–2,000 words, APA 750 pointsSunday, October 11, 8:00 PM
Week 215%

Policy Genealogy and Critical Analysis Paper

Choose one Philippine or ASEAN language-in-education policy. Trace what problem it claimed to solve, what it replaced, and who promoted or opposed it. Compare the text with the evidence on implementation and evaluate it with at least two Week 1 frameworks.

2,500–3,000 words, APA 7300-word abstractSunday, October 18, 8:00 PM
Week 315%

Language Program Plan (Draft)

For a real or proposed program in your own context: a desk-based situation analysis, a needs-analysis plan, four to six outcomes aligned to a named framework, an outline of curriculum, staffing, materials, assessment and timeline, and a draft evaluation framework with a logic model.

Structured peer feedbackSunday, October 25, 8:00 PM

The Language Policy Analysis and Program Development Portfolio moves from analysis to design to advocacy on one policy problem, and ends with a two-page policy brief to a named decision-maker. The Reflective Commentary explains your framework, your framing, and your own position as a policy actor.

15 components, see Course Guide, Section IXSunday, November 1, 8:00 PM

October 31: conference reports

The course closes with a small academic conference. There are no groups. Each of you reports alone on one strand for 30 minutes, with up to 15 slides, and then takes 15 minutes of questions from both classmates and from the professor acting as a panel of policy makers. Strand assignments will be confirmed by the professor.

Strand AStudent 1 at 1:25 PM

Philippine language-in-education policy, from the Constitution to RA 12027

Draws on Sessions 1–2. What problem did each policy shift claim to solve, what actually drove it, and what should the first three-year review of RA 12027 recommend?

Guiding questions
  1. Trace the genealogy of one shift: its texts, its promoters and opponents, and the problem it claimed to solve.
  2. Was MTB-MLE a failed policy or a failed implementation? Weigh the PIDS process evaluation (2021) and the EDCOM II Year One Report (2024).
  3. How should a "monolingual class" under RA 12027 be defined, and which learners are most likely to be left out?
Get the Strand A slide template
Strand BStudent 2 at 2:10 PM

Comparative language policy and language program planning

Draws on Sessions 3–5. Design the core of a defensible English or Filipino–English program for a Philippine institution you know, grounded in needs analysis and anchored in a named outcomes framework.

Guiding questions
  1. What can, and cannot, be borrowed from Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam, or Indonesia?
  2. Whose needs count in your needs analysis, and what are the limits of adopting CEFR descriptors in a Philippine and World Englishes context?
  3. How will your plan deal with teacher capacity, resourcing, and the tension between fidelity and local adaptation?
Get the Strand B slide template
Strand CStudent 3 at 3:05 PM

Program evaluation, critical policy analysis, and policy making

Draws on Sessions 6–8. Take one current Philippine language policy or program: evaluate whether it works, analyze how its "problem" is represented, and turn your findings into a policy brief that protects equity and language rights.

Guiding questions
  1. What logic model, indicators, and evidence would you use, and how would you weigh UNESCO's guidance against the ADBI cross-country study?
  2. What does a WPR or ethnographic reading reveal, including the role of teachers as policy arbiters?
  3. Using ODI's ROMA approach, who must be persuaded, what exactly do you recommend, and how does it protect language rights?

What every report must do

  • Answer the strand's main inquiry question and address all three guiding questions.
  • Stay within 30 minutes and 15 slides. Rehearse with a timer.
  • Rest on at least five scholarly or official sources.
  • Examine at least one concrete Philippine policy text, program, or institution.
  • End with a clear recommendation addressed to a named decision-maker.
  • When you are not reporting, bring at least two prepared questions for each classmate.

What doctoral work is judged on. A policy brief or program plan does not earn a high grade for being comprehensive or persuasive. You must show why the analysis is sound, what evidence supports it, and how the recommendation follows from it.
  • Theoretical sophistication
  • Depth and balance of engagement with scholarship and evidence
  • Rigor of policy analysis
  • Originality of contribution
  • Coherence between analysis, program design, and evaluation
  • Contextual and sociolinguistic awareness
  • Attention to equity, rights, and ethics
  • Feasibility and strategic value of recommendations
  • Scholarly and professional communication at a publishable standard

carlo.villalon@perpetualdalta.edu.ph

  • The class Facebook group chat is for quick announcements and general engagement.

Academic integrity and AI

The sources you cite must exist and say what you claim, and the argument must be yours.

  • Disclose. State at the end of each paper whether and how you used AI: the tool, the purpose, and the extent.
  • Support, not replace. AI may help you brainstorm or check clarity. The analysis and reflection must be your own.
  • Verify every source. If you cannot open it, you cannot cite it.

Confidentiality

When you write or present about your own institution:

  • Describe documents accurately, naming the document type and year.
  • Anonymize colleagues, learners, and parents.
  • Do not publish internal material you were not authorized to share.
Language policy is not only what governments write. It is what institutions require, what teachers do, and what communities believe about language, and every educator is already a policy actor.
