About the course

A doctoral course in the PhD in English program that joins two fields usually taught apart: language policy and planning, and language program development and evaluation.

This course examines how language policies are made, interpreted, contested, and implemented, and how language programs are planned, resourced, and evaluated in response to those policies.

It treats language policy and planning (LPP) as a field of research and as a field of professional action. Students study the major frameworks of the field (status, corpus, acquisition, and prestige planning; language practices, beliefs, and management; orientations to language as problem, right, and resource; the critical and ethnographic turns) and use them to analyze real policy texts and real programs.

The course gives particular attention to the Philippine language-in-education landscape, from the Bilingual Education Policy and Executive Order No. 210 to Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) and its discontinuation as a medium of instruction under Republic Act No. 12027 (2024), and places it alongside ASEAN and international experience. It also addresses the practical work of language program development: situation and needs analysis, outcomes and standards (including the CEFR and the Philippine Qualifications Framework), curriculum design, implementation, and program evaluation.

Because this is a doctoral course, students move beyond describing policies. They problematize policy texts, weigh competing evidence, design a defensible language program, and communicate recommendations to decision-makers. The course culminates in a Language Policy Analysis and Program Development Portfolio that combines a critical policy analysis, a program plan with an evaluation framework, and a policy brief.

Why it matters

Which language children learn in, which languages are tested, which varieties of English are modeled, and which languages receive resources are policy choices with consequences for learning, identity, and equity. Doctoral graduates in English are increasingly asked to lead these decisions as program chairs, curriculum developers, school leaders, policy researchers, and advisers to DepEd, CHED, and local government.

What sets it apart

None of the benchmark courses combines policy analysis and program design in a single course. PHDENG 2214 deliberately joins the two: students analyze a policy, then design and evaluate the program that would put a defensible version of it into practice.

How the course thinks

The course works at three levels throughout, and moves in a deliberate sequence from frameworks to reflection, not from policy summary to opinion to recommendation.

Level 1

Analyze

What does this policy say, where did it come from, and what framework helps us understand it?

Level 2

Critique

How is the problem represented, what evidence supports or contradicts it, and who gains or loses?

Level 3

Design and advocate

What program would put a defensible policy into practice, how would we evaluate it, and how do we persuade decision-makers?

  1. Framework
  2. Policy analysis
  3. Critique
  4. Program design
  5. Evaluation
  6. Advocacy
  7. Reflection

How it is taught

Because the students are doctoral candidates and many hold leadership positions, the course uses a seminar–studio model, not a lecture-heavy format.

  • Doctoral seminar discussion
  • Policy text close reading
  • Scholarly reading circles
  • Critical policy analysis (WPR)
  • Stakeholder role-play
  • Comparative policy jigsaw
  • Case clinics
  • Logic-model workshops
  • Needs-analysis design
  • Policy brief writing
  • Policy stakeholder forum
  • Peer review
  • Reflective and positionality writing
  • Practitioner inquiry in one's own institution

Course learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to do the following. The first four concern understanding and analyzing policy; the next three, planning, evaluating, and making it; the last three, the ethical, scholarly, and personal dimensions of that work.

  1. CLO 1
    Theoretical knowledgeCritically explain the major frameworks, models, and historical phases of language policy and planning scholarship.
  2. CLO 2
    Policy analysisAnalyze language policy texts and discourses using established analytical approaches, including Spolsky's tripartite model, Ruiz's orientations, and Bacchi's "What's the Problem Represented to be?" approach.
  3. CLO 3
    Philippine and regional contextualizationTrace the development of Philippine language-in-education policy and compare it with policies in ASEAN and other multilingual contexts.
  4. CLO 4
    Evidence evaluationEvaluate research evidence on medium of instruction, multilingual education, and English language programs, recognizing methodological strengths, limitations, and competing findings.
  5. CLO 5
    Program planningDesign a language program grounded in situation and needs analysis, with outcomes aligned to recognized frameworks (CEFR, PQF, CHED outcomes-based education, DepEd curriculum standards).
  6. CLO 6
    Program evaluationConstruct a program evaluation framework (evaluation questions, logic model, indicators, and data sources) that serves both improvement and accountability.
  7. CLO 7
    Policy making and advocacyFormulate and communicate evidence-informed policy options and recommendations to specific decision-makers and stakeholders.
  8. CLO 8
    Equity and ethicsAssess language policies and programs in terms of language rights, inclusion, Indigenous peoples' education, learners with disabilities, and social justice.
  9. CLO 9
    Scholarly contributionProduce publishable-quality scholarly writing that contributes to the field, consistent with CHED expectations for doctoral students.
  10. CLO 10
    Reflexive leadershipCritically reflect on one's positionality and agency as a language policy actor in one's own institution.

The field of study

What the course covers, the questions it keeps returning to, the Philippine policy history at its centre, and the scholars whose work it draws on.

Six major themes

Theme 1

Foundations of language policy and planning

  • Definitions and scope of LPP (Haugen, Cooper, Spolsky)
  • Status, corpus, acquisition, and prestige planning
  • Language practices, beliefs and ideologies, and management
  • Orientations: language as problem, right, and resource
  • Historical phases: classical, critical, and ethnographic LPP
Theme 2

Language ideologies, rights, and power

  • Language ideologies and attitudes
  • Linguistic human rights and minority language rights
  • English as a global language and linguistic imperialism debates
  • Indigenous languages, revitalization, and Indigenous Peoples Education
  • Filipino Sign Language and inclusive language policy
Theme 3

Language-in-education policy in the Philippines and ASEAN

  • Constitutional and statutory language provisions
  • Bilingual Education Policy and EO 210
  • MTB-MLE: design, implementation, evaluation, and discontinuation under RA 12027
  • Language policy in higher education
  • English in ASEAN; Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam, Indonesia
Theme 4

Language program planning and design

  • From policy to program: the planning cycle
  • Situation and needs analysis
  • Outcomes, standards, and frameworks (CEFR, PQF, OBE, MATATAG)
  • Curriculum, materials, teacher capacity, and assessment
  • Resourcing and implementation planning
Theme 5

Program evaluation and evidence

  • Purposes and models of language program evaluation
  • Logic models, indicators, and data sources
  • Utilization-focused and stakeholder-oriented evaluation
  • Reading and weighing evidence on medium of instruction
Theme 6

Policy analysis, policy making, and advocacy

  • The policy cycle
  • Critical policy analysis: "What's the Problem Represented to be?"
  • Ethnography of language policy
  • Research-to-policy engagement and writing policy briefs
  • Ethics, equity, and emerging issues (AI and language technologies)

Twelve guiding questions

Students return to these questions throughout the four weeks.

  1. What counts as language policy: the text, the practice, or the beliefs behind both?
  2. Who makes language policy, and who is affected by it but excluded from making it?
  3. Is language a problem to be solved, a right to be protected, or a resource to be developed, and what follows from each view?
  4. Why do well-designed language-in-education policies so often fail at the point of implementation?
  5. What does the Philippine shift from MTB-MLE to RA 12027 reveal about how evidence, ideology, and capacity interact in policy making?
  6. How should English be positioned in a multilingual nation and in ASEAN?
  7. What kind of evidence should count when language policy is decided?
  8. How do we move from a policy aim to a coherent, resourced, and assessable language program?
  9. How can a language program be evaluated so that the findings are actually used?
  10. How can language policies protect the rights of Indigenous peoples, linguistic minorities, and learners with disabilities?
  11. What is the responsible role of artificial intelligence and language technologies in language-in-education policy?
  12. What is my own agency, as an educator and scholar, in shaping language policy in my institution?

Philippine language-in-education policy, 1974 to 2025

The course reads this sequence not as policy history alone but through one question: what problem did each policy claim to solve, whose interests did it serve, what happened when it reached classrooms, and what should be done now?

  1. 1974
    Bilingual Education PolicyDepartment of Education and Culture Order No. 25 assigns Filipino to social studies, music, arts, physical education, home economics, practical arts, and character education, and English to science, mathematics, and technology.
  2. 1987
    The 1987 Constitution, Article XIV, Sections 6–9Filipino is the national language. The official languages are Filipino and, "until otherwise provided by law," English; regional languages are auxiliary official languages and auxiliary media of instruction. DECS Order No. 52 restates the bilingual policy.
  3. 2003
    Executive Order No. 210English is taught as a second language from Grade 1 and becomes the primary medium of instruction in secondary school, for not less than 70% of total time.
  4. 2009
    DepEd Order No. 74Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) is institutionalized.
  5. 2012
    DepEd Order No. 16MTB-MLE is implemented in Kindergarten to Grade 3 from SY 2012–2013, with twelve initial languages.
  6. 2013
    Republic Act No. 10533, Section 4Instruction, materials, and assessment in Kindergarten and the first three years of elementary education are to be in the regional or native language of learners. In higher education, CHED CMO No. 20 sets a general education curriculum in which Filipino and Panitikan are not required core courses.
  7. 2018
    RA 11106 and G.R. No. 216930The Filipino Sign Language Act declares FSL the national sign language of the Filipino deaf. The Supreme Court upholds RA 10533 and CMO No. 20.
  8. 2021
    PIDS process evaluation of MTB-MLEFewer than 10% of schools are found to have completed the four required preparation activities.
  9. 2024
    Republic Act No. 12027Lapsing into law on October 10, 2024, it discontinues the mother tongue as medium of instruction in Kindergarten to Grade 3 and returns to Filipino and English, with regional languages as auxiliary media. The mother tongue remains allowed in monolingual classes under conditions; DepEd must map languages within a year and review every three years.
  10. 2025
    DepEd Memorandum No. 59The implementing rules and regulations of RA 12027 are disseminated.

Academic integrity and AI

The sources you cite must exist and say what you claim, and the argument must be yours.

  • Disclose. State at the end of each paper whether and how you used AI: the tool, the purpose, and the extent.
  • Support, not replace. AI may help you brainstorm or check clarity. The analysis and reflection must be your own.
  • Verify every source. If you cannot open it, you cannot cite it.

Confidentiality

When you write or present about your own institution:

  • Describe documents accurately, naming the document type and year.
  • Anonymize colleagues, learners, and parents.
  • Do not publish internal material you were not authorized to share.
